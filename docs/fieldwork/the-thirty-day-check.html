Exhibit C · how one lead was verified

The trail behind a single row

The electrical wholesaler in Exhibit A, from the search that found it to the sheet it went out on. These steps are numbered because they happen in this order, and the order is what does the work.

  1. Searched with a one month date filter.The filter is the first cut. A business that has published nothing in a month never appears, so the dead ones are gone before any time is spent on them.
  2. Read the date off the post itself: 27 August 2026.Not "recently". The exact date, which goes in a column the client can open and check.
  3. Found two more independent sources.A Google profile and a Facebook page, all three agreeing on what the company is and all showing activity inside the window. One post is a signal. Three sources is a business.
  4. Captured every published number, not just one.The number on a Google profile is usually the shop counter. The number in the posts is usually whoever takes orders. A client who reaches the wrong one and gives up counts as a failed lead even though the business was alive.
  5. Tested it against the client's actual definition.Do they ship rather than sell over a counter. Is the freight worth a vehicle. Are they carrying the delivery cost right now. Are they a competitor. Three businesses failed this last test in week one and came off.
  6. Wrote the reason as a fact, not a pitch.No adjectives, no "great opportunity". Something the client can verify, and why it matters to somebody selling delivery.
  7. Checked it against the register of 76 names before it shipped.Names are normalised so spelling differences collapse. Two entries in week two turned out to be one business appearing under a shop name and a handle.

About twenty minutes for this lead. Some take five, some take an hour, and some take an hour and end in a drop. That is the honest shape of the work, and it is why anyone offering a thousand Accra leads for a flat fee has not done step two on any of them.

The brief

Find people who already pay to move things

The client moves goods around Accra for a living. What they wanted was not a database of companies. It was a weekly list of businesses that ship to customers often enough that delivery is already a cost on their books, with a phone number and a reason to call.

Twenty to thirty a week. Nobody sent twice.

The problem

Ghanaian business directories are full of ghosts

The client had already been burned by lists. So the first thing to establish was not where to find businesses, which is easy, but how to tell which ones still exist.

A directory entry proves nothing. Neither does a website, a follower count, or a footer showing the current year, which is generated by the software and would say 2026 on a site last touched in 2019. What proves a business is alive is a dated thing it did in public: a post, a review it answered, an update to its own listing, a price it changed.

So one rule was set, and everything else followed from it. No business goes on the sheet unless it showed a dated sign of life in the previous thirty days, and the date goes in a column so the client can check it.

What that rule cost

Thirty one of forty nine businesses in the first week failed it. Nine of them are in Exhibit B above, and every one of those looked fine.

They are not on the sheet. That is the entire service in one sentence.

Where the method was wrong

A business can be dead on one profile and busy on another

In week one a beauty wholesaler was held back. The newest thing that could be found about it was eight months old, so it failed the check and went on hold rather than on the sheet.

In week two it turned up trading hard under a separate wholesale account: posting every fortnight, four branch order lines, wholesale only pricing, shipping to more than a thousand retailers. It was the strongest prospect in the entire pool and the first check had missed it completely.

Looking for a second account before writing anything off is now part of the routine. This is on the page because a method that never reports its own misses is not a method, it is a claim.

Honest limits

What this page does not tell you

Two lists is not a track record, and the useful numbers are the ones that do not exist yet.

  • No reply rate. The client has not reported back on who was reached.
  • No conversion. Nobody knows yet how many of the forty became customers.
  • No revenue figure, so no cost per customer and no return.
  • One client, one city, one trade. Nothing here generalises yet.

Those four will be filled in from a tracker the client updates weekly, or they will not be filled in at all. What will not happen is an example being invented to sit in the gap. The whole offer is sold against exactly that, and a page that fakes its own proof has no business selling verification.

One last thing about the format

There is no email column, on purpose

Every lead list you have ever been sold has one. This market answers the phone and reads WhatsApp, and thirty six of the forty businesses delivered so far are reachable that way. An email column would make the sheet look more complete and be worth less, which is a fair description of most lead lists.
